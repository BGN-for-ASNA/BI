Testing the R2D2 Prior

The R2D2 prior turns a hard question — “what variance should each regression coefficient have?” — into an easy one: “how much of the outcome should the model explain?” This is a check that BayesForge’s implementation does what the theory promises, and a look at where the prior helps and where it quietly misleads.

  • Sparsity works. Across every setting, the 95 useless covariates are shrunk toward zero — near-zero false positives out of 95.
  • The Beta prior on R² is the load-bearing choice, and its effect reverses with noise. A cautious prior over-estimates the noise when the signal is strong; a confident prior over-estimates the signal when the noise is large.
  • Genuinely large coefficients are pulled toward zero in this V > N regime — ridge-like shrinkage, not the sharp selection of a horseshoe.
  • At N = 300 the prior-sensitivity mostly disappears — σ is recovered exactly and the “flip” is gone (step 7). What was fragility was mostly an under-determined problem.
  • For the low-R² regime of biology (0.05–0.50), drop the Dirichlet concentration to a₀ ≈ 0.1 — it restores honest intervals when the data allow it (step 8).
01

What the prior is for

Fit a linear regression with many candidate predictors and you have to say something, a priori, about the size of each coefficient. Ridge says “all small”. The horseshoe says “mostly zero, a few anything”. Both ask you to reason about the scale of an abstract regression weight — a quantity almost nobody has intuition for.

R2D2 (“R²-induced Dirichlet Decomposition”, Zhang et al. 2022) reframes the question. You place a prior on the model’s total explained variance, R² — a number every modeller can reason about — and a Dirichlet distribution splits that variance among the predictors. The coefficient priors fall out automatically.

The test problem is deliberately unforgiving:

N = 50
individuals — a small sample
V = 100
standardized, independent N(0,1) covariates — more predictors than data points
5 real effects
coefficients ±2 to ±4.4; the other 95 are exactly zero
y
= Xβ + noise, with noise standard deviation σ swept over {1, 2, 4, 8}

Standardizing the covariates is not optional. The Dirichlet splits variance; if predictors were on different scales, a coefficient’s share of that variance would be confounded with its unit of measurement.

02

The variance budget

R2D2 is a five-step generative story. Read it as a budget: a single pot of “explained variance” is opened, then divided.

  1. Set the expectation. R² ~ Beta(a, b). How much of y’s variance do you think this model explains?
  2. Open the budget. τ² = σ² R² / (1 − R²). This maps the R² belief onto a total signal variance, scaled by the residual variance σ².
  3. Split it. φ ~ Dirichlet(1,…,1) is a vector of 100 non-negative weights that sum to one.
  4. Hand out slices. λ²₃ = τ² φ₃ is predictor j’s share of the budget.
  5. Draw the coefficient. β₃ ~ Normal(0, λ²₃).
Why the split creates sparsity

The weights φ must sum to one, so the predictors compete for a fixed pot. A predictor that claims a large share leaves less for everyone else. A predictor the data don’t support is handed a tiny φ₃, its coefficient variance collapses, and β₃ is pinned near zero — without any explicit spike-at-zero. Heavy tails on the induced β prior still let a genuinely large effect through.

03

Two things called R²

The identity in step 2 only makes sense for one specific definition of R² — and it is not the one from a regression table.

model / explained-variance R²  (what the Beta targets)
R²  =  Var(Xβ) / ( Var(Xβ) + σ² )  =  τ² / ( τ² + σ² )

classical sample R²  (for reference only)
R²  =  1 − SSres / SStot

The first is a property of the data-generating process: signal variance over total variance. The second is a property of one dataset. In the simulation with σ = 1 they are 0.982 and 0.977; by σ = 8 they have drifted to 0.460 and 0.324. Throughout this report, “true R²” means the first one — the quantity the prior is actually a statement about.

Before fitting anything, it is worth looking at what each candidate Beta(a, b) actually believes about R²:

Beta(1/3, 3) — the default in the reference doc — puts almost all its mass below R² = 0.42 and expects R² ≈ 0.10. Against a process that truly explains 98% of the variance, that is a strong, wrong opinion. Beta(5, 1) is the opposite: it barely admits R² below 0.55.

04

The experiment

The inference model is the reference doc’s snippet, vectorized to 100 predictors:

# R2D2 prior, V = 100 predictors
def model(X, y):
    sigma = m.dist.exponential(1.0, name="sigma")          # sigma ~ Exp(1)
    r2    = m.dist.beta(a, b, name="r2")              # R2 ~ Beta(a, b)
    tau2  = sigma**2 * r2 / (1.0 - r2)                # open the budget
    phi   = m.dist.dirichlet(jnp.ones(V), name="phi")   # split it
    lam2  = tau2 * phi                                  # slices
    alpha = m.dist.normal(0.0, 2.0, name="alpha")
    z     = m.dist.normal(jnp.zeros(V), 1.0, name="beta_z")  # non-centered
    beta  = numpyro.deterministic("beta", z * jnp.sqrt(lam2))
    m.dist.normal(alpha + X @ beta, sigma, obs=y, name="y_obs")

Two implementation notes:

  • Non-centered by default. Writing β₃ = z₃ · λ₃ with z₃ ~ Normal(0,1) is mathematically identical to β₃ ~ Normal(0, λ²₃), but the sampler needs it. With V > N and a prior that allows R² → 1, the centered form funnels: run verbatim, Beta(3,1) gives R̂ ≈ 2.7 and thousands of divergences. Non-centered, all 20 fits land at R̂ ≤ 1.07.
  • R² is clipped to [10⁻⁴, 1−10⁻⁴] so the 1/(1−R²) term cannot blow up.

The disagreement is about the noise. Why the ranking reverses

Strong signal, cautious prior. The likelihood wants R² near 1. A Beta with all its mass near 0.1 fights that. The compromise: keep R² moderate and blame the leftover variance on noise — so σ inflates.

Weak signal, confident prior. Now the truth is R² = 0.46 but the prior expects 0.83. It pushes the budget τ² too wide; the coefficients soak up variance that is really noise; σ is left too small and R² is over-stated.

The prior does real work in both directions. A prior-predictive check on R² — the interval plot in step 3 — is the cheap way to catch a mismatch before it reaches the posterior.

Two more effects worth seeing

Shrinkage of the real effects gets worse with noise. The ratio |β̂| / |β| for the five true coefficients falls from about 0.80 at σ = 1 to about 0.45 at σ = 8 — and the 89% interval, which held 3 of 5 true values at low noise, holds only 1–2 at high noise.

The nulls finally leak — a little. At σ = 8, the confident priors produce exactly one false positive out of 95 (an 89% interval that excludes zero); the cautious Beta(1/3, 3) still produces zero. That is the trade: the cautious prior gives up power on the real effects to keep its sparsity guarantee intact.

What more data fixes: σ is recovered almost exactly and is prior-independent; the coefficients are recovered whenever true R² ≥ 0.75; the sampler is clean (zero divergences everywhere).

08

Tuning R2D2 for low R² (0.05–0.50)

Most of ecology, epidemiology and quantitative biology lives at true R² between roughly 0.05 and 0.50 — a handful of weak-to-moderate effects in a sea of noise and correlated junk. That is the regime where the plain R2D2 recipe struggles most. Here is what moves the needle, tested at true R² ≈ 0.30 (σ = 11, so the residual dominates), with the default Beta(1/3, 3).

Lever 1 — the Dirichlet concentration a₀

The reference recipe uses φ ~ Dirichlet(1, …, 1). That flat simplex is a ridge: it spreads variance evenly, which is exactly wrong when only a few predictors matter. Dropping the concentration to a₀ < 1 — φ ~ Dirichlet(a₀, …, a₀) — makes the simplex prefer sparse allocations, which gives the marginal coefficient priors heavier tails (closer to a horseshoe). This is the concentration hyperparameter from the original R2-D2 paper; BayesForge’s example just fixes it at 1.

With enough data (N = 300), a₀ ≈ 0.1 is a clear win: the intervals become honest (coverage 1/5 → 4/5), the 95 nulls are pushed from 0.17 to 0.08, and R² recovers toward the truth — all for free until a₀ ≤ 0.01, where the sparse simplex reintroduces a funnel and the sampler breaks (hundreds of divergences).

At N = 50 the problem is information, not the prior. 50 rows cannot resolve 5 moderate effects among 100 candidates when 70% of the variance is noise. Small a₀ just moves the failure around: it recovers σ and R² better while shrinking every real coefficient to zero. No prior setting rescues this cell — only more data or fewer candidate predictors.

The other levers

  • Calibrate the Beta to your field, don’t pile it at zero. Beta(1/3, 3) has infinite density at R² = 0; if your field reliably explains 10–40% of variance, a prior concentrated in that band — Beta(2, 6) or Beta(1, 3), mean ≈ 0.25 — is more honest. In the tests Beta(2, 6) and Beta(1/3, 3) performed within noise of each other, but the difference grows as the true R² rises off the floor. Best of all: fit the Beta to the R² values reported in comparable published studies (empirical Bayes).
  • Scale the outcome, or scale the σ prior. σ ~ Exponential(1) assumes y is order 1. At low R² the residual is the model, so τ² = σ²R²/(1−R²) inherits any mis-scaling of the σ prior directly. Standardize y (then σ ≈ √(1−R²) and the whole prior is scale-free), or use σ ~ Exponential(1/sd(y)) / HalfNormal(sd(y)).
  • If you can guess how many predictors matter, use a regularized horseshoe instead. Piironen & Vehtari’s version takes a target p₀ (expected number of non-zero coefficients) and controls the global scale from it — more direct than tuning a₀ when p ≫ n and you genuinely expect a spike at zero. R2D2 with a₀ < 1 is an approximation of the same idea.
  • If the effects are not sparse — many small real signals, “everything correlates with everything” — the flat Dirichlet is the right model. Then shrinking every coefficient toward zero is correct behaviour, and the only gains come from more data, grouping predictors (a Dirichlet over predictor groups), or a dimension-reduction front end (PCA / PLS).
  • Always run the prior predictive, and always report divergences and R̂. At low R² and small N the prior will dominate the posterior — that is information theory, not a bug — so the prior has to be defensible. And a₀ < 1 stiffens the geometry: keep a₀ ≥ 0.1, stay non-centered, and push target_accept up.
09

Where R2D2 bites

None of these are bugs in the implementation — they are properties of the prior, and they match the reference doc’s own “When R2D2 Fails” section.

  • Ridge, not selection. A flat Dirichlet(1,…,1) with V > N pulls every coefficient toward zero by a similar factor. It shrinks the real effects along with the noise. When you expect true sparsity and p > n, a horseshoe or spike-and-slab is better motivated.
  • Competition can starve a real effect. β89 and β93 above are genuine, mid-sized coefficients that are badly under-recovered because larger effects take the budget first.
  • Prior sensitivity scales with N⁻¹. Everything here is one dataset of 50. The likelihood cannot outvote a confident, wrong Beta. In a larger sample the priors would converge.
  • The centered parameterization is not usable here. It has to be non-centered; the verbatim doc snippet needs that substitution for any prior that admits R² → 1.
  • Divergences are non-trivial. Even non-centered, at target_accept = 0.95 the harder fits show 1–5% divergent transitions. The geometry is stiff; the posteriors are usable but the sampler is working hard.
  • Normal outcomes only. The identity τ² = σ²R²/(1−R²) needs a free residual variance. It does not exist for Binomial, Poisson, or Categorical likelihoods.
10

How to use it well

  • Do the prior-predictive check on R² first. Sample your Beta(a, b), look at the 5th–95th percentile against what you plausibly expect. This is the single most effective safeguard.
  • Small sample + suspected strong fit ⇒ do not use the Beta(1/3, 3) default. It will inflate σ and shrink real effects. Widen it (Beta(1, 1)) or shift it up.
  • When in doubt, be less confident, not more. A wrong-but-vague prior degrades gracefully; a wrong-but-confident one (Beta(5, 1) here) produces the largest errors when the truth disagrees.
  • Low true R² (0.05–0.50) — drop the Dirichlet concentration to a₀ ≈ 0.1–0.5. The flat Dirichlet(1,…,1) is a ridge; a₀ < 1 gives horseshoe-like tails that keep the intervals honest and crush the null noise (see step 8). Do not go below a₀ = 0.1 without reparameterizing — the sampler funnels.
  • Calibrate the Beta to your field. If R² is reliably 0.1–0.4, use a prior concentrated there (Beta(2, 6)), or fit Beta(a,b) to the R² of comparable published studies — don’t default to a prior with all its mass at zero.
  • Always standardize predictors — and consider standardizing y. The Dirichlet weights only mean “share of variance” if the predictors are comparable; scaling y also makes σ ~ Exp(1) scale-appropriate, which matters most when the residual dominates.
  • Use the non-centered parameterization. It is in the test script by default; keep it.
  • Report divergences and R̂. This prior can look fine in a summary table and still have a stressed sampler underneath — especially with a₀ < 1 or in the R² → 1 region.
